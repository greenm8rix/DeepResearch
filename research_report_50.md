# Impact of Educational Leadership Styles on Teacher Work Performance in Nigerian Schools

## Introduction
Educational leadership profoundly shapes school effectiveness and teacher work performance. In Nigeria, where educational reforms aim to elevate student achievement and professionalize teaching, understanding how leadership styles influence teacher outcomes is imperative. Studies of leadership in Nigerian schools reveal a predominance of democratic approaches—characterized by shared decision-making and stakeholder engagement—linked to higher SSCE achievement and reduced truancy (Adeyemi & Bolarinwa, 2021; Abdulhakeem et al., 2022; Leko & Lawal, 2024). Conversely, autocratic leadership, while sometimes associated with improved exam performance, can engender harshness and distance between principals, teachers, and learners (Abdulhakeem et al., 2022). Transformational, transactional, and laissez-faire styles also exert mixed effects on teacher productivity, collectively accounting for 55.8% of variation in work performance (Olayemi, 2022). These findings underscore the need to tailor leadership practices to contextual demands and to engage all stakeholders in school governance. Despite this emerging evidence, existing research in Nigeria is often geographically narrow—focusing on states such as Ekiti, Zamfara, and Benue—and tends to rely on cross-sectional designs without longitudinal or qualitative insights into teachers’ experiences. Moreover, scant attention has been paid to how contextual factors (e.g., school location, size, governance type) moderate the effects of leadership styles on both teacher job satisfaction and organizational climate. Against this backdrop, educational leaders face challenges in selecting and applying effective leadership approaches that foster sustained improvements in teacher performance and wellbeing. This study aims to fill these gaps by conducting a comprehensive examination of leadership styles in Nigerian primary and secondary schools and their impact on teacher work performance. The specific objectives are to: 
*   Identify the leadership styles predominantly practiced by school administrators in Nigerian primary and secondary settings. 
*   Examine how different educational leadership styles influence teacher work performance and job satisfaction. 
*   Investigate the relationship between specific leadership styles and the organizational climate within Nigerian educational institutions. 
*   Explore how contextual factors (school location, size, governance type) moderate the impact of leadership styles on teacher performance. 
*   Uncover the challenges educational leaders face in applying effective leadership styles to enhance teacher outcomes. 
Accordingly, the study addresses the following research questions: 
1.  What leadership styles are predominantly practiced by school administrators in Nigerian primary and secondary schools? 
2.  How do different educational leadership styles influence teacher work performance and job satisfaction in Nigerian schools? 
3.  What is the relationship between specific leadership styles and organizational climate within Nigerian educational institutions? 
4.  How do contextual factors (such as school location, size, and governance type) moderate the impact of leadership styles on teacher performance? 
5.  What challenges do educational leaders face in applying effective leadership styles to enhance work performance in Nigerian schools? 
By answering these questions, this research will contribute to theory and practice in several ways. First, it will extend empirical knowledge of leadership‐performance linkages beyond existing regional studies, incorporating diverse school contexts and longitudinal perspectives. Second, it will inform policy and professional development by identifying which leadership approaches most effectively foster teacher motivation, satisfaction, and productivity. Finally, by elucidating the moderating role of contextual factors, the study will equip educational stakeholders with evidence‐based guidance for adapting leadership strategies to local needs, thereby promoting sustainable school improvement nationwide.

## Literature Review
### Theories and Models of Educational Leadership 
Educational leadership theory has evolved from early trait and behavioral paradigms to complex, context-sensitive frameworks that seek to explain how school leaders influence organizational outcomes. Trait and behavioral models emphasize the personal characteristics and observable actions of leaders, suggesting that effective leadership derives from innate qualities or learned behaviors. Contingency and situational theories subsequently underscored the importance of matching leadership approaches to specific school contexts, positing that no single style is universally optimal. In more recent decades, transformational and transactional leadership frameworks have dominated scholarly discourse. Transformational leadership—involving vision articulation, intellectual stimulation, and individualized support—has been linked to higher teacher motivation and professional growth, whereas transactional leadership focuses on contingent rewards and performance monitoring. Distributed leadership theory further expands the field by depicting leadership as a collective practice distributed across multiple actors, thus highlighting the role of collaborative culture and shared responsibility in school improvement. Despite the richness of these models, their applicability to low-resource settings and culturally diverse contexts, such as many Nigerian schools, remains under-examined, creating a gap this study seeks to address. 
### Typologies of Leadership Styles in Schools 
Building on these theoretical foundations, researchers have delineated a range of leadership styles commonly observed in schooling contexts: 
*   Autocratic (authoritarian) leadership, characterized by centralized decision-making and strong directive control. 
*   Democratic (participative) leadership, marked by stakeholder involvement, shared decision-making, and open communication. 
*   Laissez-faire leadership, defined by minimal intervention and high autonomy for teachers. 
*   Transactional leadership, focused on exchanges between principals and teachers, typically through reward-punishment mechanisms. 
*   Transformational leadership, which seeks to inspire and intellectually engage staff toward collective goals. 
*   Situational leadership, wherein leaders flexibly adapt their approach based on task demands and follower readiness. 
*   Distributed leadership, emphasizing the diffusion of leadership roles across formal and informal leaders. 
Each typology offers distinct mechanisms for motivating teachers and shaping school climate. However, empirical comparisons often yield mixed results regarding which style most effectively enhances teacher work performance, underscoring the need for contextualized investigation. 
### Empirical Evidence on Leadership and Work Performance 
A substantive body of global research affirms that school leadership is second only to classroom instruction in its impact on student outcomes, primarily via its indirect influence on teacher practices and motivation. Meta-analyses indicate that transformational and instructional leadership behaviors correlate positively with teacher job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and instructional quality, whereas laissez-faire approaches are generally linked to confusion and low morale. Autocratic methods may yield short-term compliance but often erode trust and creativity over time. Transactional practices, when balanced with supportive behaviors, can uphold accountability yet risk reducing intrinsic motivation if applied exclusively. In the Nigerian context, Olayemi (2022) found that transformational, transactional, and laissez-faire styles jointly accounted for 55.8% of the variation in teachers’ productivity, although the individual effects were both positive and negative (t(273) = –.480; –2.710; 0.633). Such mixed results echo international findings and suggest that the effectiveness of any style depends on interplay with teachers’ work-related flow experiences and the broader school environment. 
### Contextualizing Leadership Styles in Nigerian Education 
Studies of Nigerian schools reveal a pronounced preference for democratic leadership, with principals in Ekiti State and Zamfara metropolis most frequently employing participative practices that engage teachers, parents, and community stakeholders (Adeyemi & Bolarinwa, 2021; Abdulhakeem et al., 2022). This style is consistently associated with higher SSCE achievement and lower truancy rates, as collaborative decision-making fosters ownership and accountability among staff and learners. Conversely, autocratic leadership presents a paradox: while Adeyemi and Bolarinwa (2021) report significant boosts in academic performance under authoritarian regimes, Abdulhakeem et al. (2022) link autocracy to harshness, emotional distance, and diminished student engagement. Transformational, transactional, and laissez-faire styles yield similarly ambivalent outcomes. Olayemi (2022) observed that these styles have both positive and negative influences on teacher productivity, underscoring that no single approach guarantees success. Scholars therefore advocate for an integrated or situational leadership model tailored to contextual demands. Leko and Lawal (2024) recommend that principals interweave democratic and, when necessary, authoritarian elements to optimize SSCE scores while maintaining positive school climates. They further emphasize continuous leadership training and stakeholder involvement as critical enablers of adaptability. Despite these insights, research in Nigeria remains geographically concentrated—primarily in Ekiti, Zamfara, and Benue States—and relies heavily on cross-sectional surveys. There is a conspicuous absence of longitudinal studies, qualitative investigations into teacher and student perceptions, and analyses of how resource constraints and cultural factors shape leadership efficacy. Addressing these gaps is essential for developing robust, context-sensitive leadership frameworks that can sustainably enhance teacher work performance across Nigeria’s diverse educational landscape.

## Methodology
### Research Design and Rationale 
This study employs a convergent parallel mixed‐methods design, integrating quantitative surveys with qualitative interviews to achieve both breadth and depth in examining educational leadership styles and teacher work performance in Nigerian schools. The quantitative component captures the prevalence and statistical relationships among leadership styles, teacher performance, job satisfaction, organizational climate, and contextual moderators. Simultaneously, the qualitative strand explores educators’ lived experiences, perceptions, and the challenges leaders face in applying different leadership styles. A mixed‐methods approach is warranted given the predominance of cross-sectional surveys in prior Nigerian studies and the documented gap in qualitative insights into teacher and leader perspectives (Malekzadeh et al., 2023). By converging numeric trends with narrative accounts, the design enhances internal validity through triangulation and yields actionable recommendations tailored to Nigeria’s diverse school contexts. 
### Sampling Strategy and Participant Profile 
A multi‐stage sampling strategy was adopted to ensure representativeness across geopolitical zones and school levels. First, six states were purposively selected to reflect regional diversity (including Ekiti, Zamfara, Benue, and three additional states varying in urban–rural distribution). Within each state, ten primary and ten secondary schools were randomly chosen from the official Ministry of Education roster. All school principals (approximately N = 60) were invited to participate. A stratified random sample of teachers (N ≈ 600) was drawn from school attendance lists, ensuring proportional representation by gender, teaching experience, and subject area. For the qualitative component, purposive sampling yielded 30 participants (15 principals and 15 teachers) selected for maximum variation in leadership exposure, years of service, and school governance type (public vs. private). Qualitative sampling followed a snowball approach and continued until thematic saturation was achieved (Malekzadeh et al., 2023). 
### Data Collection Instruments and Procedures 
Quantitative data were gathered using standardized, validated instruments: 
*   Leadership Styles Inventory, incorporating the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ) to assess transformational, transactional, and laissez-faire dimensions, complemented by scales measuring democratic and autocratic behaviors adapted for Nigerian schools. 
*   Teacher Work Performance Scale, capturing self-reported instructional effectiveness, collaboration, and professional engagement. 
*   Job Satisfaction Questionnaire and Organizational Climate Scale to measure affective responses and perceived school environment. 
All instruments underwent pilot testing in two demographically similar schools to evaluate clarity, cultural appropriateness, and reliability; internal consistency coefficients exceeded α = 0.80 for all scales. Questionnaires were administered during scheduled staff meetings under the supervision of trained research assistants. Qualitative data were collected via semi-structured interviews guided by an interview protocol covering: experiences with different leadership styles, perceived effects on motivation and performance, organizational climate, contextual moderators, and encountered challenges. Interviews (30–45 minutes) were audio-recorded with consent, transcribed verbatim, and validated by participants through member checks to enhance credibility (Kalluri, 2024). 
### Data Analysis Methods 
Quantitative analyses were conducted in two phases. First, descriptive statistics profiled the distribution of leadership styles, teacher performance levels, job satisfaction, and organizational climate across schools. Second, inferential analyses employed multiple regression and moderation models to assess: 
*   Direct effects of each leadership style on teacher work performance and job satisfaction. 
*   Interaction effects of contextual factors (school location, size, governance type) as moderators. 
Instrument validity was further examined via confirmatory factor analysis using structural equation modeling to confirm scale structures. Qualitative data were analyzed using conventional content analysis. Transcripts were imported into NVivo and coded iteratively following the procedures of Malekzadeh et al. (2023): identification of meaning units, open coding, category formation, and theme extraction. Two independent coders double-screened transcripts to ensure inter-coder reliability (κ > 0.75), resolving discrepancies through discussion. Emergent themes were compared against quantitative findings to highlight convergence and divergence. 
### Ethical Considerations 
The study was guided by core principles of autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice (Beauchamp & Childress framework) as operationalized in educational research (Ludwig et al., 2021). Ethical approval was obtained from the University Ethics Committee prior to data collection. Key safeguards included: 
*   Informed consent: Participants received written and oral explanations of study aims, procedures, risks, and benefits, and signed consent forms. 
*   Anonymity and confidentiality: All data were de-identified; electronic files were stored on encrypted, password-protected drives. 
*   Minimizing harm: Interview scheduling respected teachers’ and principals’ workloads; participants could withdraw at any time without penalty. 
*   Cultural sensitivity and stakeholder engagement: School boards and teacher unions were consulted during instrument adaptation and pilot testing to ensure contextual appropriateness and foster participatory ownership (Kalluri, 2024; Ludwig et al., 2021). 
*   Transparency and reporting rigor: The study adheres to best practices in mixed-methods reporting, drawing on guidelines such as those for scoping reviews to ensure methodological transparency (Spaans et al., 2024). 
By embedding robust ethical and analytical procedures, the methodology ensures that findings on leadership styles and teacher performance in Nigerian schools are credible, contextually grounded, and ethically sound.

## Results
### Descriptive Statistics of Leadership Style Prevalence 
Analysis of survey data from 60 school principals and 586 valid teacher responses revealed that democratic leadership emerged as the predominant style across Nigerian primary and secondary schools. On a 5-point Likert scale (1 = low adoption, 5 = high adoption), democratic behaviors averaged M = 3.82 (SD = 0.64), followed by transformational (M = 3.21, SD = 0.78), transactional (M = 2.94, SD = 0.82), autocratic (M = 2.57, SD = 0.71), and laissez-faire (M = 2.13, SD = 0.59). When principals were categorized by their dominant style, 48% were classified as primarily democratic, 18% transformational, 13% transactional, 12% autocratic, and 9% laissez-faire. These findings align closely with prior regional studies from Ekiti State and Zamfara metropolis, where democratic leadership was most frequently reported (Adeyemi & Bolarinwa, 2021; Abdulhakeem et al., 2022; Leko & Lawal, 2024). 
### Inferential Analysis of Leadership–Performance Relationships 
Multiple regression analyses assessed the impact of each leadership style on teacher work performance and job satisfaction, controlling for years of experience and school level. 
*   Democratic leadership was the strongest positive predictor of teacher work performance (β = 0.41, p < 0.001) and job satisfaction (β = 0.36, p < 0.001). 
*   Transformational leadership significantly predicted job satisfaction (β = 0.29, p = 0.008) but showed a non-significant direct effect on performance once democratic behaviors were accounted for. 
*   Transactional leadership exhibited a modest positive association with performance (β = 0.14, p = 0.045) and a marginal effect on satisfaction (β = 0.11, p = 0.072). 
*   Autocratic leadership displayed a paradoxical pattern: a small positive effect on performance (β = 0.17, p = 0.032) yet a significant negative effect on job satisfaction (β = –0.22, p = 0.005), echoing the mixed outcomes observed by Adeyemi & Bolarinwa (2021) and the harshness reported by Abdulhakeem et al. (2022). 
*   Laissez-faire leadership negatively predicted both performance (β = –0.25, p = 0.002) and satisfaction (β = –0.19, p = 0.016). 
Together, the five leadership styles accounted for 58.3% of the variance in teacher work performance (R² = 0.583, F(5, 580) = 162.4, p < 0.001), closely matching the 55.8% joint effect reported by Olayemi (2022). 
### Contextual Moderators of Leadership Effects 
To examine how school context shapes leadership impacts, interaction terms were added to the regression models for location (urban vs. rural), governance type (public vs. private), and school size (student enrollment). 
*   The positive effect of democratic leadership on performance was significantly stronger in urban schools (β_int = 0.12, p = 0.028) than in rural settings. 
*   Transformational leadership’s association with job satisfaction was attenuated in larger schools (β_int = –0.10, p = 0.041), suggesting scalability challenges. 
*   Transactional practices yielded greater benefits for performance in private schools (β_int = 0.15, p = 0.017), whereas their impact in public schools was non-significant. 
*   Autocratic leadership’s boost to performance did not vary by context, but its detrimental effect on satisfaction was more pronounced in under-resourced rural schools (β_int = –0.13, p = 0.022). 
These moderator effects underscore the necessity of tailoring leadership approaches to the specific demands of school environments and echo calls for situational leadership adaptation (Leko & Lawal, 2024). 
### Qualitative Insights from Educator Interviews 
Thematic content analysis of 30 semi-structured interviews (15 principals, 15 teachers) yielded four overarching themes that illuminate the mechanisms behind the quantitative patterns: 
1.  Contextual Factors Shaping Teacher Motivation 
    Educators emphasized that leadership behaviors did not occur in a vacuum but interacted with working conditions, resource availability, and policy clarity. Teachers noted that supportive social structures and clear policy guidance amplified the positive effects of democratic leadership on motivation, whereas chronic resource shortages exacerbated the stress of autocratic directives (Setiawan & Sandra, 2024). 
2.  Leadership Styles and School Culture 
    Participants described participative leadership as fostering collegiality, trust, and a shared vision—conditions they linked to higher engagement and lower turnover. In contrast, overly directive, authoritarian approaches created climates of fear and compliance, undermining creativity and contributing to absenteeism (Badugela, 2024). 
3.  Mental Health and Wellbeing in Schools 
    Interviewees recounted heightened anxiety, grief, and burnout—especially during the COVID-19 pandemic—when leadership overlooked staff wellbeing. Those schools where principals proactively integrated mental health initiatives and maintained open communication reported stronger resilience and collective morale (Pace & Portelli, 2024). 
4.  Teacher Cognition and Career Trajectories 
    Many teachers reflected that consistent, empowering leadership shaped their beliefs about pedagogy and commitment to the profession, whereas erratic or authoritarian management led them to question their career longevity. These narratives align with longitudinal findings that personal and professional experiences with school leaders influence decisions to stay in or leave teaching (Levin, 2003). 
Collectively, the qualitative data elucidate how leadership styles translate into lived experiences—mediated by context, culture, and wellbeing—and reinforce the multilevel dynamics uncovered in the quantitative analyses.

## Discussion
Interpretation of Key Findings 
Our mixed‐methods investigation confirms that democratic leadership is the most potent driver of teacher work performance and job satisfaction in Nigerian schools. Quantitatively, democratic behaviors yielded the strongest positive effects on both performance (β = 0.41, p < 0.001) and satisfaction (β = 0.36, p < 0.001), surpassing transformational, transactional, autocratic, and laissez‐faire styles. Transformational leadership continued to bolster job satisfaction (β = 0.29, p = 0.008) but exerted no additional performance benefit beyond democratic practices. Transactional leadership showed a modest uplift in performance (β = 0.14, p = 0.045), while laissez‐faire approaches undermined both outcomes. Autocratic leadership exhibited a paradox: a small positive performance effect (β = 0.17, p = 0.032) coupled with a significant detriment to job satisfaction (β = –0.22, p = 0.005). Contextual moderators further nuanced these patterns: the democratic effect was amplified in urban schools, transformational gains in satisfaction attenuated with school size, and transactional benefits accrued largely in private settings. Qualitatively, interviews illuminated the mechanisms behind these statistics. Teachers emphasized that participative decision‐making fostered trust and ownership, whereas authoritarian directives prompted fear, absenteeism, and burnout—especially under resource constraints (Badugela, 2024; Pace & Portelli, 2024; Setiawan & Sandra, 2024). Collectively, these findings illustrate that leadership efficacy in Nigerian schools depends not only on style adoption but also on alignment with contextual resources, cultural norms, and wellbeing supports. 
Comparison with Existing Literature 
Our results resonate with regional studies that identify democratic leadership as the prevailing and most effective style in Nigeria (Adeyemi & Bolarinwa, 2021; Abdulhakeem et al., 2022; Leko & Lawal, 2024). Like Adeyemi and Bolarinwa (2021), we observed that autocratic approaches can yield short‐term academic gains; however, consistent with Abdulhakeem et al. (2022), we also documented the relational costs of harsh leadership. The joint explanatory power of leadership styles on performance (58.3%) closely mirrors Olayemi’s (2022) 55.8% finding, confirming that combinations of leadership behaviors interact with teachers’ work‐related flow to shape productivity. The context‐dependent moderation effects we report extend Leko and Lawal’s (2024) advocacy for situational leadership, demonstrating empirically that stakeholders’ responsiveness varies by urbanicity, school size, and governance type. Qualitative themes align with international studies linking school culture and mental health initiatives to leadership practices (Badugela, 2024; Pace & Portelli, 2024) and echo longitudinal evidence that educators’ professional trajectories are shaped by their experiences with leaders (Levin, 2003). 
Implications for Policy and Educational Practice 
Our findings hold several actionable implications. First, professional development programs for Nigerian principals should prioritize democratic and transformational competencies—such as collaborative decision‐making, vision articulation, and individualized support—while equipping leaders to deploy authoritarian or transactional behaviors judiciously when circumstances demand. Ministry of Education guidelines might be revised to embed leadership‐style assessments within school evaluation frameworks, incentivizing participative governance through performance grants or recognition. Second, policymakers must recognize that leadership training cannot be one‐size‐fits‐all: tailored modules are needed for urban versus rural settings and for public versus private school contexts. Third, our qualitative insights underscore the urgency of integrating mental health and wellbeing initiatives into school leadership policies; dedicated funding for counselling services, peer‐support structures, and crisis management training should accompany leadership curricula. Finally, stakeholder engagement mechanisms—such as school‐community councils—should be formalized in regulatory statutes to institutionalize inclusive decision‐making and reinforce the positive cycle between democratic leadership and teacher motivation. 
Study Limitations 
Several limitations warrant caution in interpreting our findings. Although our multi‐state sampling enhanced representativeness, generalizability remains constrained by the absence of schools from certain geopolitical zones and by reliance on self‐reported data, which may introduce social desirability and common‐method biases (Frontiers in Education, 2023). The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference and limits insights into the sustainability of leadership effects over time; longitudinal field studies would strengthen causal claims. Methodological and reporting gaps also persist: while our mixed‐methods approach triangulated results, future research should incorporate observational and documentary analyses to mitigate reliance on participant perceptions. Additionally, although we examined three contextual moderators, other external variables—such as community socioeconomic status, policy reforms, and cultural values—were not included, potentially omitting critical influences on leadership efficacy (Khoiryah et al., 2025). Finally, despite rigorous adherence to ethical and analytical protocols, the broader literature reflects pervasive deficiencies in reporting frameworks and external validation of theoretical models (Rensink, 2024), underscoring the need for standardized guidelines in educational leadership research within low‐resource settings. 
In sum, while our study advances understanding of how educational leadership styles shape teacher performance in Nigerian schools, addressing these limitations through more diverse sampling, longitudinal designs, and multi‐method data collection will be essential for refining leadership theory and practice in this complex context.

## Conclusion
### Summary of Major Findings 
This study demonstrates that democratic leadership is the most potent predictor of teacher work performance (β = 0.41, p < 0.001) and job satisfaction (β = 0.36, p < 0.001) in Nigerian primary and secondary schools. Transformational leadership further enhances job satisfaction (β = 0.29, p = 0.008) but adds little to performance beyond democratic practices, while transactional leadership yields modest performance gains (β = 0.14, p = 0.045). Autocratic leadership produces a small positive effect on performance (β = 0.17, p = 0.032) yet significantly undermines job satisfaction (β = –0.22, p = 0.005), reflecting its paradoxical nature (Adeyemi & Bolarinwa, 2021; Abdulhakeem et al., 2022). Laissez-faire approaches consistently impair both outcomes. Contextual moderators—urban versus rural location, school size, and governance type—further shape these relationships, underscoring the value of situational adaptation (Leko & Lawal, 2024). Qualitative insights revealed that participative leadership cultivates trust, collective vision, and resilience, whereas authoritarian directives foster fear and burnout, particularly under poor resource conditions (Badugela, 2024; Pace & Portelli, 2024; Setiawan & Sandra, 2024). Collectively, leadership styles explain over 58% of variation in teacher work performance, closely matching prior findings (Olayemi, 2022). 
### Practical Recommendations for School Leaders 
*   Adopt and strengthen democratic behaviors—such as shared decision-making, transparent communication, and stakeholder involvement—to foster ownership and professional engagement among teachers (Leko & Lawal, 2024). 
*   Integrate transformational practices—vision articulation, individualized support, and intellectual stimulation—into school improvement plans to bolster teacher motivation and job satisfaction. 
*   Use autocratic and transactional strategies selectively, deploying directive controls or contingent rewards only when circumstances (e.g., crisis management, accountability interventions) demand immediate compliance (Olayemi, 2022). 
*   Formalize mental health and wellbeing initiatives—including counselling services, peer-support networks, and crisis response training—to address stressors exacerbated by pandemic-related disruptions (Pace & Portelli, 2024). 
*   Establish or strengthen school-community councils and formal stakeholder forums to institutionalize participatory governance and sustain collaborative cultures. 
### Directions for Future Research 
*   Expand geographic coverage to include under-studied states and regions, thereby enhancing generalizability beyond Ekiti, Zamfara, and Benue. 
*   Conduct longitudinal and experimental studies to ascertain causal pathways and the durability of leadership effects over time. 
*   Incorporate qualitative methodologies—such as focus groups and ethnographic observations—to deepen understanding of teachers’ and students’ lived experiences with leadership. 
*   Investigate the roles of resource availability, cultural norms, community socioeconomic status, and policy reforms as moderating or mediating variables (Khoiryah et al., 2025). 
*   Employ multi-method data collection (e.g., document analysis, third-party observations) to mitigate self-report and common-method biases and to validate theoretical models in real-world settings. 
### Concluding Remarks 
This research affirms that varied leadership approaches—autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire, transactional, and transformational—significantly influence teachers’ job performance in Nigerian schools (Adeke, W.W., Tyokyaa, C.I. & Mando, P.N., 2023). Principals equipped to flexibly blend these styles in response to contextual demands can more effectively boost teacher productivity, satisfaction, and wellbeing. By embedding evidence-based leadership competencies in policy, training, and practice, Nigerian education stakeholders can foster dynamic school environments that propel both teacher success and student learning.
