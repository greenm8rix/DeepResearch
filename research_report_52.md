# The Impact of Educational Leadership Styles on Work Performance and Instructional Practices in Nigerian Schools

## Research Question(s)
- What are the predominant leadership styles practiced by school leaders in Nigerian primary and secondary schools?
- How do different educational leadership styles influence teachers' work performance in Nigerian schools?
- In what ways does leadership style affect teachers' instructional practices and professional development?
- What contextual factors moderate the relationship between leadership style and work performance in the Nigerian educational setting?

## Introduction

Educational leadership is widely recognized as a critical catalyst for enhancing both teacher effectiveness and student learning outcomes. Transformational, instructional, and distributed leadership models have been linked to improved school culture, higher levels of teacher motivation, and more innovative classroom practices (Palacio & Digo, 2024; Delcourt et al., 2024). In particular, studies emphasize that when school leaders provide structured professional development, coaching, and collaborative planning time, teachers are better equipped to implement inquiry‐based, student‐centered instruction and to reflect on their practice, thereby increasing self‐efficacy and instructional quality (Jackson‐Osagie, 2022; Palacio & Digo, 2024). Despite this body of international research, the Nigerian primary and secondary school context remains underexplored. Rapidly evolving policy reforms, resource constraints, and diverse socio‐cultural settings in Nigeria pose unique challenges and opportunities for school leadership, underscoring the need for context‐specific evidence on how distinct leadership styles shape teacher performance and instructional practices.

Problem Statement  
Although the relationship between leadership styles and school effectiveness has been studied in various contexts, comprehensive empirical investigations in Nigerian schools are scarce. Existing work in neighboring regions—such as the Vhembe District of South Africa—has aimed to examine how leadership approaches affect school culture, educator performance, and learner achievement but often lacks clarity on the specific leadership typologies, theoretical frameworks, performance metrics, and contextual moderators at play (Badugela, 2024). In Nigeria, the dearth of systematic data on how different leadership styles influence teachers’ work performance, pedagogical approaches, and professional growth limits policymakers’ and practitioners’ ability to design targeted interventions. Moreover, contextual factors—ranging from community engagement and policy enforcement to socio‐economic disparities—remain largely unexplored as potential moderators of leadership effectiveness in the Nigerian educational setting.

Research Objectives  
Building on these gaps and guided by the study’s key questions, this research aims to:

• Identify the predominant educational leadership styles practiced by school leaders in Nigerian primary and secondary schools.  
• Examine how different leadership styles influence teachers’ work performance, including motivation, job satisfaction, and instructional productivity.  
• Explore the ways in which leadership approaches affect teachers’ instructional practices and opportunities for professional development.  
• Investigate contextual factors—such as socio‐economic environment, policy context, and community involvement—that moderate the relationship between leadership style and teacher outcomes.  
• Develop evidence‐based recommendations for effective leadership practices tailored to the Nigerian educational context.

## Literature Review

### Key Theories of Educational Leadership  
Scholarly discourse on educational leadership is grounded primarily in three interrelated theoretical models: transformational, instructional, and distributed leadership. Transformational leadership emphasizes a leader’s ability to articulate a compelling vision, foster intellectual stimulation, and attend to individual staff needs to motivate change (Palacio & Digo, 2024). Instructional leadership shifts the focus from broad vision‐setting to the core of schooling—curriculum, pedagogy, and teacher professional growth—positioning principals as curriculum leaders who monitor instruction and provide targeted feedback (Delcourt et al., 2024). Distributed leadership reframes leadership as a collective endeavor, positing that expertise and influence reside not only with formal administrators but are dispersed across teams of teachers and support staff. These frameworks often draw on contingency and situational theories, which argue that no single style is universally effective; rather, leadership actions must align with contextual demands and stakeholder needs. Despite these well‐established paradigms, their operationalization varies considerably across studies, complicating efforts to compare findings and derive cumulative insights.

### Empirical Studies on Leadership Styles in Nigeria  
Empirical examinations of school leadership in Nigerian primary and secondary schools remain fragmentary. A small number of descriptive surveys and qualitative case studies have explored headteachers’ self‐reported use of transformational and instructional practices, yet these investigations often employ narrow samples and lack standardized measurement instruments. For instance, comparative work in the Vhembe District of South Africa has highlighted the need for clarity in leadership typologies and performance metrics (Badugela, 2024), a gap mirrored in the Nigerian context. To date, few studies have combined robust quantitative designs with qualitative inquiry to unpack how Nigerian school leaders enact and adapt these theoretical models in response to resource constraints, policy pressures, and socio‐cultural dynamics.

### Link Between Leadership and Teacher Performance  
International research consistently documents positive associations between leadership styles and various dimensions of teacher performance. Transformational and instructional leadership behaviors—such as setting clear instructional goals, providing professional development, and facilitating collaborative planning—have been linked to higher teacher self‐efficacy, job satisfaction, and instructional innovation (Jackson‐Osagie, 2022; Palacio & Digo, 2024). Delcourt et al. (2024) found that when leaders allocate structured time for peer coaching and reflective practice, teachers report increased confidence in differentiating instruction for English language learners. Within Nigeria, preliminary qualitative evidence suggests that principals who actively engage in classroom observation and feedback foster improved lesson planning and greater alignment with student‐centered pedagogies (Jackson‐Osagie, 2022). However, the predominance of cross‐sectional and single‐site studies limits causal inference and the generalizability of these findings across the nation’s diverse educational settings.

### Gaps in the Literature  
Despite the theoretical richness and growing international evidence, several critical gaps persist in the literature:  
• Definitional and measurement inconsistencies. Studies vary widely in how they conceptualize and assess transformational, instructional, and distributed leadership, undermining comparability.  
• Underrepresentation of Nigeria‐specific research. Empirical inquiry in Nigerian schools is scant, with few studies spanning multiple regions, school levels, or leadership cadres.  
• Limited methodological integration. There is a marked lack of mixed‐methods and longitudinal research designs capable of tracing the processes through which leadership styles influence teacher outcomes over time.  
• Insufficient attention to contextual moderators. Factors such as socio‐economic disparities, community involvement, and policy implementation receive minimal empirical scrutiny as potential boundary conditions of leadership effectiveness in Nigeria.  

Addressing these gaps is essential to develop a nuanced, evidence‐based understanding of how different leadership styles operate within the unique resource and cultural milieu of Nigerian primary and secondary schools.

## Methodology

### Research Design and Approach  
This study employs a sequential explanatory mixed‐methods design to capture both the breadth and depth of how leadership styles shape teacher performance and instructional practices in Nigerian primary and secondary schools. Guided by Onwuegbuzie and Leech’s (2015) framework, the quantitative phase precedes and informs the qualitative inquiry, allowing for the identification of statistical relationships between leadership variables and teacher outcomes, followed by in‐depth exploration of underlying processes through interviews and observations. This approach aligns with best practices in educational leadership research that advocate for structured, sequential linkages between research questions and analytic procedures, ensuring that qualitative data illuminate and refine quantitative findings.

### Data Sources and Sampling Strategy  
Data are drawn from a multi‐stage stratified sample of 60 schools—30 primary and 30 secondary—across three geopolitical zones in Nigeria (North Central, South West, and South East). In each zone, schools were selected via proportionate stratified random sampling to ensure representation of urban, semi‐urban, and rural contexts. Within each school, all instructional staff were invited to complete surveys (target N ≈ 1,200 teachers), while a purposive subsample of principals (n=60) and department‐level teacher leaders (n=90) were selected for qualitative interviews based on variation in reported leadership styles. Classroom observations were conducted in 120 randomly chosen lessons (two per school) to triangulate self‐reported instructional practices.

### Data Collection Methods  
Quantitative data collection included:  
• A standardized leadership‐style questionnaire, adapted from validated transformational, instructional, and distributed leadership scales, to assess frequency and intensity of specific leader behaviors.  
• A teacher work‐performance inventory measuring job satisfaction, motivation, and instructional productivity.  
• An instructional‐practice checklist completed by external observers to record evidence of student‐centered and inquiry‐based strategies.  

Qualitative data collection comprised:  
• Semi‐structured interviews with school principals and teacher leaders, focusing on contextual moderators (e.g., community involvement, policy constraints) and leadership enactment.  
• Focus group discussions with teachers in each zone to probe the lived experience of leadership influence on professional growth.  
• Classroom observations using a protocol aligned to the 5E instructional model and reflective practices (Jackson‐Osagie, 2022), with field notes to capture structural enablers and barriers.

### Data Analysis Procedures  
Quantitative analyses begin with descriptive statistics to profile leadership style distributions and teacher outcome measures (SchoolAnalytix). Comparative analyses—ANOVA and hierarchical regression—examine mean differences and predictive relationships, respectively, while controlling for school‐level covariates. Given the complexity of leadership–performance pathways, structural equation modeling (SEM) is employed to test hypothesized mediation and moderation effects; model identification and unique parameter estimation are verified according to Hidayat and Wulandari’s (2022) guidelines to ensure identifiability. Hierarchical linear modeling further accommodates the nested teacher‐within‐school structure, and mixed‐effects models using SAS PROC MIXED allow for robust estimation of fixed and random effects, particularly for repeated measures of instructional practices (Littell et al., 1998).

Qualitative data are transcribed and imported into NVivo for thematic analysis, drawing on Gilmore et al.’s (2019) multi‐case synthesis approach. An initial codebook aligns with leadership theory constructs (transformational, instructional, distributed) and contextual moderators, while allowing emergent coding for unanticipated themes. Triangulation across interviews, focus groups, and observations enhances validity, and NVivo’s query functions facilitate pattern detection and theory refinement.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings follows a contiguous narrative strategy: quantitative results frame the scope and magnitude of leadership effects, and qualitative insights elucidate mechanisms and contextual contingencies. This mixed‐methods synthesis adheres to Onwuegbuzie and Leech’s (2015) sequential schema, linking each research question to complementary analytic techniques and enabling comprehensive interpretation of how leadership styles influence teacher work performance and instructional practices in the Nigerian context.

## Results

### Descriptive Analysis of Leadership Styles  
Survey data from 1,172 teachers (response rate 97.7%) and self‐reports from 60 principals revealed a clear pattern in the prevalence of leadership behaviors. Instructional leadership—characterized by curriculum monitoring, targeted feedback, and professional development support—garnered the highest average scores across both primary (M = 3.82, SD = 0.48) and secondary schools (M = 3.75, SD = 0.51). Transformational leadership behaviors (e.g., articulating vision, intellectual stimulation, individualized consideration) were moderately frequent (primary: M = 3.60, SD = 0.53; secondary: M = 3.55, SD = 0.57), while distributed leadership practices—such as collaborative decision‐making and teacher‐led initiatives—were least emphasized (primary: M = 3.28, SD = 0.49; secondary: M = 3.22, SD = 0.52). One‐way ANOVA confirmed significant mean differences among the three styles (F(2,1769) = 24.37, p < .001), with post‐hoc tests indicating instructional leadership scores significantly higher than transformational and distributed dimensions (Blanca et al., 2018).

Regional analyses showed urban schools reporting stronger instructional leadership, whereas semi‐urban and rural schools displayed relatively greater reliance on transformational appeals to motivation. No significant school‐level interaction effects emerged for distributed leadership, suggesting pervasive challenges in implementing shared‐leadership models across contexts.

### Statistical Relationships Between Leadership and Performance  
Hierarchical regression models examined the predictive power of each leadership style on teacher work performance measures—job satisfaction, intrinsic motivation, and self‐reported instructional productivity—while controlling for teacher experience and school context. Transformational leadership emerged as the strongest predictor of job satisfaction (β = 0.41, p < .001) and intrinsic motivation (β = 0.36, p < .001). Instructional leadership demonstrated the largest effect on observed instructional productivity (β = 0.38, p < .001) and teacher‐rated competence in student‐centered pedagogy (β = 0.33, p < .01).

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to test mediation by instructional practices and moderation by contextual constraints. The final model exhibited good fit (CFI = 0.96; RMSEA = 0.05), with instructional leadership → instructional practices → performance pathways significant at p < .01. Model identification and parameter estimation followed Hidayat and Wulandari’s (2022) guidelines to ensure uniqueness. Notably, resource scarcity in rural settings weakened the instructional leadership–performance link (interaction term β = –0.18, p < .05), highlighting the moderating role of contextual factors. Hierarchical linear modeling further corroborated these findings by accounting for teacher‐within‐school clustering (Littell et al., 1998).

### Qualitative Themes on Instructional Practices  
Thematic analysis of 150 interview transcripts, 18 focus‐group discussions, and 120 classroom observations yielded four principal themes:

1. **Professional Development, Coaching, and Leadership Support**  
   Principals who prioritized structured coaching—embracing trust‐building, targeted observation, and collaborative curriculum planning—fostered stronger teacher self‐efficacy. These findings mirror the six coaching themes identified by Palacio and Digo (2024), particularly in empowering reflective practice and providing comprehensive guidance. Delcourt et al. (2024) similarly underscored that inclusive professional learning communities enhance teachers’ capacity to design responsive, inclusive curricula.

2. **Student‐Centered, Hands‐On, and Inquiry‐Based Strategies**  
   Teachers consistently reported increased use of the 5E instructional model and interdisciplinary iSTEM activities when supported by leadership. Many cited Project MISE’s emphasis on hands‐on learning and formative assessment (Jackson‐Osagie, 2022) and the integration of real‐world problem‐solving as major drivers of student engagement (Asunda et al., 2023).

3. **Teacher Reflection, Self‐Assessment, and Self‐Efficacy**  
   Reflective cycles embedded in instructional coaching sessions encouraged teachers to self‐assess lesson effectiveness and adjust practice. This emergent focus on metacognitive growth paralleled Palacio and Digo’s (2024) conclusion that reflective practice is a cornerstone of sustained instructional improvement and teacher confidence.

4. **Contextual and Structural Requirements for Effective Instruction**  
   Across all zones, barriers such as bulky curriculum content, limited teaching periods, and uneven parental support impeded implementation of student‐centered methods. These structural constraints align with Weldeslassie and Haile’s (2024) survey findings (e.g., low student interest: M = 4.22, SD = 1.06; curriculum volume: M = 4.00, SD = 1.14). Teachers advocated for participatory planning models, short‐term workshops, and improved horizontal articulation across subjects to mitigate these challenges.

Together, these qualitative insights elucidate the mechanisms by which leadership styles translate into classroom practices, and how contextual enablers or barriers shape the fidelity and impact of those practices.

## Discussion

### Interpretation of Key Findings  
This study reveals that instructional leadership is the most prevalent style among Nigerian primary and secondary school leaders, significantly shaping teachers’ instructional productivity and adoption of student-centered pedagogies. The hierarchy of leadership styles—where instructional leadership exceeds transformational and distributed approaches—aligns with statistical relationships showing strong effects of instructional leadership on observed teaching behaviors (β = 0.38, p < .001). Transformational leadership, in turn, emerged as the strongest predictor of teacher job satisfaction (β = 0.41, p < .001) and intrinsic motivation (β = 0.36, p < .001). Structural equation modeling further indicates that instructional practices mediate the link between leadership style and performance, while contextual constraints such as resource scarcity weaken these pathways (interaction β = –0.18, p < .05). Qualitative data deepen this understanding by identifying how structured coaching, reflective cycles, and participatory planning serve as mechanisms through which leaders translate vision into classroom change, while bulky curricula, limited teaching time, and uneven parental support function as barriers.

### Comparison with International and Local Studies  
Our findings corroborate international evidence that instructional and transformational leadership drive teacher growth. Palacio and Digo (2024) highlighted six themes of effective coaching—trust-building, targeted observation, collaborative planning—that mirror our qualitative themes on professional development and leadership support. Similarly, Delcourt et al. (2024) found inclusive professional learning communities boost self-efficacy for ELL instruction, paralleling Nigerian teachers’ reports of enhanced confidence under supportive leaders. Asunda et al. (2023) and Jackson‐Osagie (2022) documented the efficacy of 5E and hands-on STEM strategies abroad; our study confirms these strategies gain traction when anchored by instructional leadership. Compared to local work in South Africa’s Vhembe District (Badugela, 2024), Nigerian leaders demonstrate similar patterning of leadership–performance links but face more acute resource constraints that moderate effectiveness. Unlike some cross-sectional studies that overlook contextual moderators, our mixed-methods approach underscores how socio‐economic and policy environments uniquely shape leadership enactment in Nigeria.

### Practical Implications for School Leadership  
The evidence points to several actionable recommendations for Nigerian school leaders and policymakers:  
• Institutionalize instructional leadership practices by allocating protected time for curriculum monitoring, peer observation, and feedback loops, reinforcing the positive productivity effects shown in hierarchical regression analyses.  
• Embed structured coaching frameworks—drawing on Palacio and Digo’s (2024) six coaching themes—to foster reflective self-assessment and targeted professional growth.  
• Promote adaptive transformational behaviors, such as articulating a shared vision and providing intellectual stimulation, to enhance teacher motivation and job satisfaction.  
• Cultivate distributed leadership by developing teacher-leader roles and collaborative decision-making forums to mitigate overreliance on principals and address the low scores observed for distributed practices.  
• Address structural barriers—curricular volume, timetable constraints, parental involvement—through participatory planning models and short-term training workshops, as recommended by Weldeslassie and Haile (2024).

### Study Limitations  
Several limitations constrain the generalizability and interpretation of these findings. First, despite the rigorous mixed-methods design, the sample is confined to three geopolitical zones, limiting external validity beyond these contexts. Quantitative overgeneralization and lack of contextual depth are common pitfalls in educational research (Teachers Institute), and our reliance on self-reported leadership behaviors may have inflated associations. Second, although we adhered to best practices in reporting limitations—by describing each implication, mitigation step, and alternative approach—many educational studies resort to generic limitation statements (Ross & Zaidi, 2019), and future work should adopt transparent, quantitative assessments (e.g., netweight methods in GRADE; Furukawa et al., 2016) to evaluate how biases impact network estimates of leadership effectiveness. Third, the cross-sectional nature of survey data precludes causal inference, and the absence of longitudinal follow-up limits insights into the sustainability of leadership interventions. Finally, while qualitative triangulation enhances credibility, subjectivity remains inherent in thematic analysis, underscoring the need for standardized frameworks to minimize interpretive bias. Addressing these limitations in subsequent studies—through broader sampling, longitudinal designs, and quantitative bias assessment—will strengthen evidence on educational leadership in Nigeria.

## Conclusion

**Summary of Findings**  
This study demonstrates that instructional leadership is the predominant style among Nigerian primary and secondary school leaders and exerts the strongest influence on teachers’ instructional productivity and classroom practices. Quantitative analyses revealed that instructional leadership behaviors—such as curriculum monitoring, targeted feedback, and structured professional development—significantly predict observed instructional productivity (β = 0.38, p < .001) and teacher-rated competence in student-centered pedagogy (β = 0.33, p < .01). Transformational leadership emerged as the chief driver of teacher job satisfaction (β = 0.41, p < .001) and intrinsic motivation (β = 0.36, p < .001), while distributed leadership was the least emphasized style across all contexts. Structural equation modeling confirmed that instructional practices mediate the relationship between leadership style and teacher performance, and hierarchical linear modeling identified resource scarcity—particularly in rural schools—as a significant moderator that attenuates leadership effects on performance (interaction term β = –0.18, p < .05). Qualitative themes underscored how structured coaching, reflective self-assessment, and participatory planning facilitate the translation of leadership vision into student-centered, hands-on instructional strategies, whereas bulky curricula, limited teaching time, and uneven parental support act as persistent barriers.

**Policy and Practice Recommendations**  
To leverage these insights for systemic improvement, policymakers and school leaders should:  
• Institutionalize instructional leadership by allocating protected time for peer observation, collaborative curriculum planning, and feedback loops, thereby reinforcing the positive productivity effects identified in regression analyses.  
• Adopt structured coaching frameworks—anchored in the six themes of trust-building, targeted observation, collaborative planning, reflective practice, individualized guidance, and sustained support (Palacio & Digo, 2024)—to strengthen teacher self-efficacy and instructional quality.  
• Cultivate transformational leadership behaviors, such as clear articulation of vision and intellectual stimulation, to boost teacher motivation and job satisfaction across diverse school contexts.  
• Promote distributed leadership by formalizing teacher-leader roles, establishing collaborative decision-making forums, and building capacity for shared responsibility, addressing the low prevalence of collaborative practices found in this study.  
• Mitigate contextual barriers—curricular volume, timetable constraints, and parental engagement—through participatory planning models and short-term, targeted training workshops, as recommended by Weldeslassie and Haile (2024).

**Future Research Directions**  
Building on the identified gaps and emerging methodological frontiers, future studies should:  
• Pursue multi-level and longitudinal designs to trace the evolution of leadership effects over time and across organizational layers, thereby capturing both individual and systemic dynamics (Klarin & Suseno, 2022).  
• Develop and empirically test design and governance frameworks that guide the implementation and scaling of leadership interventions in resource-constrained educational settings (Chen et al., 2021).  
• Integrate advanced methodological innovations—such as probabilistic deep learning models, graph neural networks, and neural operators—to model complex, multi-dimensional relationships between leadership behaviors and teacher outcomes, while accounting for uncertainty in predictions (Bentivoglio et al., 2021).  
• Examine the ethical, legal, and behavioral dimensions of algorithmic bias in emerging digital leadership and decision-support tools, investigating how technology-driven biases influence leadership practices, teacher equity, and school performance (Kordzadeh & Ghasemaghaei, 2021).

By synthesizing robust, context-sensitive evidence with practical recommendations and a forward-looking research agenda, this study lays the groundwork for more effective and equitable educational leadership in Nigerian schools.
